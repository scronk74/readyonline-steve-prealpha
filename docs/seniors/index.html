The problem is not your ability. It is the pressure.

A scam works by rushing you. It brings fear or good news, a voice that sounds official, and a reason to keep it secret. That works on people of every age.

And many older adults see it coming. In 2025, people aged 60 and over sent about 470,000 fraud reports to the US Federal Trade Commission. About 3 out of 4 of those reports (74%) did not report any money lost.

So this page doesn't tell you to be more careful. You already are. It gives you one habit for the moment someone pushes.

Source: US Federal Trade Commission, Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026 (October 2026). The figures count reports that gave an age, not people.

Option A: Straight to it

One habit: Pause · Think · Act

For any call, text, email or pop-up you didn't expect:

  1. Pause: hang up. Or close the window, or put the phone down. Hanging up is not rude. Urgency is a reason to pause.

  2. Think. Who is really asking? What do they want: money, a code, a password, or a way into your computer? Did you start this, or did they?

  3. Act: check another way. Use a phone number or website you already trust: the number on your bank card, a bill, or your own address book. Never use a number or a link they gave you.

Then tell someone you trust what happened. Saying it out loud often makes it clear.

Trust the feeling that something is off. And when everything feels fine but someone wants money or a code, check anyway.

A real business gives you time to check. Slow is a superpower.

Six common scams

Most of these tricks are old. The phone, the screen and the voice are new. The same three steps work on all of them.

1. An urgent call from "your bank"

What it looks like. A caller says they are from your bank's fraud team. Your account is at risk, they say, and you must act now. They may know your name and part of your card number. Your phone may even show the bank's name. That can be faked. Then they ask you to move your money to a "safe account", or to read out a code that was just sent to your phone.

What to do. Hang up. Call the number on your bank card. Never move money to "keep it safe" because a caller says so. Never read out a code to someone who called you.

2. A new romance

What it looks like. Someone you met online is warm and kind, and writes every day. Things move fast. They can't meet in person; there is always a reason. Then there is a problem: a hospital bill, a plane ticket, a fee to release some money. Or there is a tip about an investment. They ask you to keep it between the two of you.

What to do. Wanting connection is human and good. Real connection does not need secrecy or money. Don't send money, gift cards or account details to someone you have only met online. Talk to someone you trust before you send anything.

3. A pop-up that says your computer has a virus

What it looks like. A warning fills your screen. It may make a loud noise, and it may use the name of a company you know. It tells you to call a number. The person who answers asks to connect to your computer from far away. That is called remote access. Then they ask you to pay.

What to do. Don't call the number. A pop-up does not know your device is in danger, and a real warning never asks you to call a phone number. Close the window. If it won't close, turn the computer off and on again. Never give remote access to someone who contacted you first. If you want the computer checked, ask someone you know and trust, or a shop you choose yourself.

4. A link in a text or email

What it looks like. A message says a parcel is waiting, a toll is unpaid, or your account is locked. It gives you a link to tap, often with a deadline. Messages like this are sometimes called phishing. A sender name can be copied. A link can lead somewhere different than it appears.

What to do. Don't tap the link. Use the company's app, or type the address yourself. If the message was real, you'll find it there.

5. A profile that isn't who it says

What it looks like. A friend request comes from a stranger who seems to live nearby, or to know your family. Or a second request comes from a friend you already have: that account may be a copy. Or a friend's account sends you something odd: a link, a request for money, or "send me the code you just got".

What to do. Friend requests can be ignored without being rude. Check with the real person another way: phone them. Keep your phone number and address private until you know who someone is.

6. A voice or a video that may be faked

What it looks like. The phone rings, and it sounds just like your grandson. He's in trouble, he needs money right now, and he begs you not to tell his parents. Computer programs can now copy a voice from a short recording. They can also make a video of a famous person saying things that person never said, such as praising an investment.

What to do. Don't try to judge the voice. Hang up, and call the person back on the number you already have. If they don't answer, call someone else in the family. Agree on a family word ahead of time: a word only your family knows, which a caller must say before anyone sends money.

What they all share

  • Hurry. "Act now."

  • Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone." "Stay on the line."

  • An odd way to pay. Gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency (digital money such as Bitcoin), or cash handed to a courier.

One sign is enough to hang up.

Option B: A story

The call at ten past four

A made-up story, built from a scam that is common.

Ruth is 74. She ran a school office for thirty years, and not much got past her.

At ten past four the phone rings. The screen shows the name of her bank.

"Mrs. Carter, this is the fraud team. Someone is using your card in another city right now. We need to move your savings to a safe account before five o'clock."

He is polite. He knows her name and the last four numbers on her card. Her heart is going fast.

Sign: hurry.

"Please stay on the line," he says. "And don't mention this to the staff at your branch. One of them may be involved."

Sign: secrecy.

Ruth has met this before. Forty years ago it was a man at the door who had to fix the roof today, for cash. The costume is new. The push is the same.

Pause. "I'll call you back," she says, and she hangs up while he is still talking.

Think. She didn't call him. He called her. And he wants her to move her money and tell no one.

Act. She turns her bank card over and calls the number on the back. It takes a few minutes. Her account is fine. Nobody at the bank called her.

Then she phones her neighbor Dale to tell him. Dale got the same call last week. He hung up too, and he hadn't thought to mention it to anyone.

Ruth wasn't lucky. She had a habit: hang up, think, check another way.

If Ruth had already moved the money, her next step would be the same: call the bank on the number on her card, and tell someone she trusts. It would not be her fault.

Social media: keep it yours

Social media is good at keeping people close: family photos, old friends, local news. Keep using it. A few habits keep it yours.

  • Choose who sees you. Privacy settings let you choose who sees you. Set your posts to "friends only", and hide your friends list if you can.

  • Ignore requests you didn't expect. A profile can pretend to be local or familiar. If a friend you already have sends a second request, tell them another way. Their account may have been copied.

  • Check surprising messages. If a friend's account asks for money, a code or a favor, phone them. Someone else may be using their account.

  • Look up a shop before you buy from an ad. Search for the shop's name yourself, with the word "complaints".

  • Keep your savings out of it. Don't let someone you met on social media guide where you put your money, however well it seems to be going. A famous face in an ad can be faked.

  • Enjoy new people, and keep the check. A new friend online can be real. A real friend doesn't need secrecy or money.

Learning online, at your own pace

Nobody knows everything about a phone or a computer. You only need the next thing: a video call with family, a class, a recipe, a form.

  • Learn from places you choose. A public library, a senior center or a community class often teaches these skills. So can a patient friend or relative.

  • Try video lessons and online classes. There are good ones on almost any subject. Go to them yourself: search for the name, or type the address.

  • Be wary of help that finds you. A real teacher or helper doesn't call, text or pop up first.

  • Read the small print on "free". Some free trials ask for a card, then charge every month. Before you type a card number, find out what it costs later and how to cancel.

  • Take your time. A real class will still be there tomorrow. "Sign up today or lose your place" is hurry, and hurry is a sign of pressure.

  • Ask again. Asking the same question twice is how everyone learns this.

Opening and reading a message is rarely the problem. The risk is in what comes next: tapping a link, calling a number, or sending money.

Pass it on

You have spent a lifetime learning to spot a push. That is worth sharing.

  • Tell one person about one scam. A neighbor, a friend, someone in your family.

  • If you hang up on a scam call, mention it. Your friends and neighbors may be getting the same call.

  • Tell your family your rule: "If I ever seem to call you in a panic, asking for money, hang up and call me back."

Hearing about a scam ahead of time helps. In a 2019 study of 1,408 people who had reported a scam, 9% of those who had already heard of that scam lost money. Among those who hadn't heard of it, 34% did.

Source: FINRA Investor Education Foundation, BBB Institute for Marketplace Trust and Stanford Center on Longevity, Exposed to Scams (2019). A survey in the United States and Canada.

More steps if it has already happened

You are not the first, and it is not your fault. The people who do this are practiced at it.

  1. Stop all contact. Don't answer their calls or messages. Don't pay a "fee" to release money you already sent.

  2. Call your bank or card company. Use the number on your card or on a statement. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment if they can. Sometimes they can, and sometimes they can't. It is always worth asking, and sooner is better.

  3. Paid another way? If you bought gift cards, keep the cards and the receipt, and call the company named on the card. If you used a wire service or a payment app, call that company.

  4. Shared a password or a code? Change that password now. Change it anywhere else you use the same one.

  5. Let someone connect to your computer? Don't use it for banking or shopping until someone you trust has checked it. Then change your passwords.

  6. Tell someone you trust. You could start with: "Something happened with a phone call, and I'd like your help." If the first person isn't much help, try someone else.

  7. Watch for a second scam. Someone may call and offer to get your money back for a fee. Don't pay it. That is usually the same kind of people, back again.

You can also report what happened. Reports help warn other people. The Get help page shows where.

Losing money this way can hit hard. If it is weighing on you, the Get help page also has someone to talk to.
