Seniors

Your wisdom is still the asset.

You already learned to question a rushed call, a prize letter, or an offer that sounds too good to be true. This page updates those instincts for texts, apps, email and AI: computer programs that can now copy a voice or a face.

If you've already sent money or shared details

Someone used pressure and lies on you. That is on them, not on you. It happens to careful people every day.

  1. Stop. Don't reply, and don't send anything more.

  2. Call your bank or card company now. Use the number on your card or on a statement. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment if they can. Sometimes they can, and sometimes they can't. Sooner is better.

  3. Tell someone you trust. You don't have to sort this out alone.

The problem is not your ability. It is the pressure.

A scam works by rushing you. It brings fear or good news, a voice that sounds official, and a reason to keep it secret. That works on people of every age.

Many older adults see it coming. In 2025, people aged 60 and over sent about 470,000 fraud reports to the US Federal Trade Commission. About 3 out of 4 of those reports (74%) did not report any money lost.

Source: US Federal Trade Commission, Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026 (October 2026). The figures count reports that gave an age, not people.

One habit: Pause · Think · Act

For any call, text, email or pop-up you didn't expect:

  1. Pause: hang up. Or close the window, or put the phone down. Hanging up is not rude. Urgency is a reason to pause.

  2. Think. Who is really asking? What do they want: money, a code, a password, or a way into your computer?

  3. Act: check another way. Use a phone number or website you already trust: the number on your bank card, a bill, or your own address book. Never use a number or a link they gave you.

A real business gives you time to check. Slow is a superpower.

Six common scams

Most of these tricks are old. The phone, the screen and the voice are new.

If you getDo this
A call from "your bank" about fraudHang up. Call the number on your card.
A new friend or romance online who needs moneyDon't send it. Talk to someone you trust first.
A pop-up that says "virus" and gives a numberDon't call it. Close the window.
A text or email with a linkDon't tap it. Use the app, or type the address yourself.
A friend request you didn't expect, or a friend's account asking for moneyIgnore it. Phone the real person.
A voice like family, in troubleHang up. Call them back on the number you have.

What they all share

  • Hurry. "Act now."

  • Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone." "Stay on the line."

  • An odd way to pay. Gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency (digital money such as Bitcoin), or cash handed to a courier.

One sign is enough to hang up.

Pass it on

You have spent a lifetime learning to spot a push. That is worth sharing.

  • Tell one person about one scam: a neighbor, a friend, someone in your family.

  • Tell your family your rule: "If I ever seem to call you in a panic, asking for money, hang up and call me back."

More steps if it has already happened

You are not the first, and it is not your fault. The people who do this are practiced at it.

  • Shared a password or a code? Change that password now, everywhere you use it.

  • Let someone connect to your computer? Don't use it for banking or shopping until someone you trust has checked it.

  • Watch for a second scam. Someone may call and offer to get your money back for a fee. Don't pay it.

  • You can report what happened. Reports help warn other people. The Get help page shows where.

Losing money this way can hit hard. If it is weighing on you, the Get help page also has someone to talk to.
