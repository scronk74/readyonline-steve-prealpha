Get help

Parents

Connection is the safety feature.

You don't need to know every app. You need your child to know one thing: they can tell you. So make them a promise, in your own words, before your family needs it:

"If something goes wrong online, tell me. You won't be in trouble for telling. I won't take your phone away for telling. I'll stay calm, and I'll listen first."

This site tells young people to find someone they trust. It can't promise them how that person will react. Only you can.

Not a parent? If a child or teen might come to you, this page is for you too.

If something has already happened

Your child told you, or you found out another way. Start with your first minute. The plan can wait.

  1. Pause. Put down what you're doing. Take one breath before you speak.
  2. Think. What does my child need from me right now? Not: how did this happen?
  3. Act. Say: "Thank you for telling me. You're not in trouble. We'll sort this out together." Then listen.

Found out another way? Start with: "You're not in trouble. I'm worried, and I want to help."

If someone is in danger right now, call your local emergency number.

What not to say

Your child may already be blaming themselves. Some first lines close the door. Each one has a swap that keeps it open.

Instead of Try
"Why did you do that?" "I'm glad you told me. Take your time."
"I told you this would happen." "This isn't your fault. Someone used pressure or a trick on you."
"Give me your phone." "You're keeping your phone. Can we look at it together?"
"It's not a big deal. Just ignore it." "I can see this is hard. I believe you."
"Who is it? I'm going to message them." "We won't reply to them. Let's work out the next step together."

Already said one of these? Fear does that. You can repair it: "I got that wrong. I was scared. Thank you for telling me. Can we start again?"

Next steps, together

Do these with your child, not for them. Ask before each one: "Is it okay if we…?"

  1. Let them tell it their way. You don't need every detail tonight.
  2. Stop the contact. No more replies. No payments. Nothing more sent.
  3. Keep the messages and the account name. Don't delete the chat.
  4. Block and report the account in the app. Let your child do the tapping if they want to.
  5. Don't contact the other person yourself.
  6. Say what happens next. If you need to bring in someone else, such as the school, another parent or the police, tell your child first. Don't promise to keep it secret. Do promise: "I won't do anything behind your back."
  7. Check in tomorrow. "How are you doing after yesterday?" One talk is rarely the whole of it.

If an account or a setting needs to change, change it together and say why. That is a safety step, not a punishment.

If a rule got broken, come back to it later, calmly. Tonight, the telling is what matters.

Why the promise works

Young people told us. In ReadyOnline's 2026 survey, 80 people aged 14 to 22 were asked what would help them ask an adult for help. They could pick more than one answer.

What they said would help How many of the 80 Your half of it
Knowing they won't get in trouble 80% "You won't be in trouble for telling."
An adult who will stay calm 62% "I'll stay calm."
An adult who will listen before reacting 59% "I'll listen first."
Not losing their phone straight away 42% "I won't take your phone away for telling."

None of the four is an app or a setting. All four are about how an adult reacts.

Taking the phone can turn a request for help into a reason to stay silent.

It's a small survey, not a national one, and nobody under 14 was asked.

A promise you can keep

  • It covers the telling. You aren't promising there will never be rules. You are promising that telling you is always safe.
  • The phone stays. Safety steps are still fine: blocking an account, changing a setting. Decide them together, and say why.
  • It can't make your child tell you. It makes telling easier. Your child may go to another adult first: an aunt, a coach, an older cousin. That counts. The plan is that they tell someone.
  • Say it more than once. Once at a calm moment. Again when a news story or a friend's trouble gives you an opening.
  • If you break it, repair it. "I got that wrong. Can we start again?"

Option A: Straight to it

The words for the conversation

Make the promise

Pick a calm moment: in the car, on a walk, over a meal. Not in the middle of an argument about screen time. Keep it short. A shrug is a fine answer.

"If something goes wrong online, tell me. You won't be in trouble for telling. I won't take your phone away for telling. I'll stay calm, and I'll listen first."

Then add: "If you'd rather tell someone else first, that's okay. Who would that be?"

Everyday openers

Easy questions, asked often, make the hard talk less strange.

  • "What are you enjoying online at the moment?"
  • "Show me how that game works."
  • "Has anything online ever felt weird or wrong?"
  • "If something went wrong, which two grown-ups could you tell?"
  • "What would make it easier to tell me?"

What your child may say first

You may hear You can say
"Something online feels wrong. Can you help me?" "Yes. I'm glad you told me. Show me, and we'll look together."
"Can I show you something on my phone? I'm not sure about it." "Sure. Thanks for showing me. Let's look."
"Something happened online. Can you just listen first?" "Okay. I'm listening." Then hold your questions until they've finished.
"I need help with something online, and I need you to stay calm." "I will. Take your time."

Three lines to know by heart

  • "Thank you for telling me."
  • "You're not in trouble."
  • "We'll sort this out together."

If you only remember one, make it the first.

If they don't want to talk yet

Don't push. Leave the door open: "Okay. You can always talk to me, at any time. And if it's easier, you can tell someone else you trust."

Option B: Step by step

The first minute, step by step

A made-up family. This is the parent's side of "The free skin", the walk-through in the 13 to 14 section of the Young people page.

  1. Weeks before: the promise.

    Dana is driving her son Leo, 13, to practice. She says: "If something goes wrong online, tell me. You won't be in trouble for telling. I won't take your phone away for telling. I'll stay calm, and I'll listen first." Leo shrugs. "Okay."

    What this shows: It took twenty seconds, and a shrug was enough. The promise is made before anyone needs it.

  2. Tuesday night: the opening line.

    Leo stands in the kitchen doorway. "Something happened online. Can you just listen first?"

    What this shows: The Young people page teaches that line. It asks for one thing: listen first.

  3. Pause.

    Dana's stomach drops. Ten questions arrive at once. She puts down the dish towel and takes a breath. "Okay. I'm listening."

    What this shows: The first response decides whether the door stays open.

  4. Listen.

    Someone in a game offered Leo a free skin, an outfit for his game character. They needed his login "for two minutes". He gave it. The skin never came, and his coins are gone. Dana doesn't interrupt. She doesn't ask why.

    What this shows: Let them tell it their way. "Why did you…?" sounds like blame, even when you don't mean it that way.

  5. Think.

    What does Leo need right now? Not a lecture. He already feels foolish. He needs to know that telling her was the right call.

    What this shows: Children are often blaming themselves already. Your job in this minute is to take blame off the table.

  6. Act: the words.

    "Thank you for telling me. You're not in trouble. Someone tricked you on purpose, and that's on them. We'll sort this out together."

    What this shows: Thanks first. Then no blame. Then "together". Reassurance comes before questions or consequences.

  7. Next steps, together.

    Leo holds the phone, and Dana sits beside him. They change the password, there and in the two other places he used it. They report the player in the game and block the account. The phone stays with Leo.

    What this shows: The promise holds when it is tested. Fixing it together is a safety step, not a punishment.

  8. The next day.

    "How are you doing after yesterday? I'm glad you told me." On the weekend they talk about a family rule: logins stay private, even from friends.

    What this shows: One talk is rarely the whole of it. The rule comes later, and calmly, after the telling has been thanked.

If Dana had snapped first, her next step would be a repair: "I got that wrong. I was scared. Thank you for telling me. Can we start again?" It is never too late to say that.

What pressure looks like online

You cannot be expected to understand every platform and scam pattern alone. Many adults feel unsure. In ReadyOnline's 2026 survey of 153 adults, about half of those who rated themselves said they were not confident, or only slightly confident, that they could spot grooming (47% of 122) or sextortion (47% of 125).

  • Grooming is when someone builds a child's trust, step by step, in order to take advantage of them.
  • Sextortion is blackmail. Someone threatens to share a private photo or video, real or faked, unless the child pays or sends more.

You don't need to spot everything. The Young people page teaches three signs, and they are the same in every app.

  • Hurry. "Do it now or you'll miss out." A real offer gives you time to check.
  • Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone." Secrecy is a red flag, even when someone is kind.
  • Owing. "After everything I gave you." A gift, a game credit or a streak gets used to make a child feel they owe something. A streak is a count of the days in a row that two people have messaged each other.

One sign is enough to pause.

Three more things worth knowing:

  • It often starts kind. Compliments, gifts, attention. Being kind doesn't cancel the signs.
  • It isn't only strangers. It can be someone your child knows: a friend of a friend, someone from school, an ex.
  • It often moves. "Let's talk on another app." A private chat takes away the people who could help your child notice pressure.

What you might notice at home

  • More secrecy about their phone, or about who they are talking to
  • New things, game credits or money they can't explain
  • Pulling away from friends or activities, or new friends you hear little about
  • Much more time online than usual, or suddenly not wanting to go online

Any of these can be an ordinary part of growing up. A sign is a reason to talk, not proof of anything.

Pick your moment, and say why you're asking: "You've seemed quiet lately. I'm not checking up on you, and you're not in trouble. Is anything online bothering you?"

Settings that help: coming

Settings and conversation work together. In ReadyOnline's 2026 survey of 153 adults, 83% said a combination of monitoring, restrictions and education works best.

Coming: step-by-step setup guides for apps and phones. Each one will show the date it was last checked. The first ones planned are for Snapchat, and for the screen-time settings on iPhone and Android.

Until then, two things hold for any setting:

  • A setting can limit who contacts your child and what they see. It can't make them tell you when something goes wrong. The promise is what makes telling easier.
  • Set it up with your child where you can. Tell them what you turned on, and why.

Supporting an older relative

You may be doing this work on two fronts: for your children, and for a parent or grandparent.

The signs are much the same. Connection is human. Secrecy, urgency, and requests for money are the signals to pause.

So is the promise. An older relative may worry about more than the money: about being judged, or about losing their say. You can offer: "If something ever goes wrong, tell me. I'll help, and it stays your money and your decision."

When they do tell you, start where you would with your child: "I'm glad you told me."

More steps for three situations

If someone threatens to share a photo or video of your child

Someone has a nude or private photo or video of your child, real or faked, or says they do. They say they'll share it unless your child pays or sends more. This is blackmail. It's often called sextortion. It can happen in any family.

Say this first: "This is not your fault. The person threatening you is the one doing wrong. I'm on your side."

Then, together:

  1. Don't pay, and don't send more. Giving in rarely makes it stop.
  2. Stop replying. Don't reply for them, either.
  3. Keep the messages and the account name. Don't delete the chat. Don't save or forward the images. You don't need to see the photo or video to help.
  4. Block the account and report it in the app.
  5. Go to Get help. It has a route for this: "Someone wants images, or is threatening to share them".
  6. Stay close. A young person who is being threatened can feel trapped. Keep them company tonight. If they seem overwhelmed, or talk about hurting themselves, go to Get help now: "I need to talk to someone now".

Your child doesn't need perfect proof to get help, and doesn't have to explain everything first.

The Young people page has these steps in a young person's words: If someone threatens to share a photo or video of you

If they shared a password or a login code

Change that password now, together. Change it anywhere else they use the same one.

If they sent money, or gave card or bank details

Go to Get help: "I think I've been scammed". If your card or bank account was used, call your bank on the number on your card. Ask what they can do. Sometimes they can help, and sometimes they can't. Sooner is better.

This is hard on you, too

Finding out can bring fear, anger or guilt. None of that makes you a bad parent. You can't see everything, and you didn't cause this. The person who used pressure did.

The Get help page also has someone to talk to. That is for you as well as for your child.
