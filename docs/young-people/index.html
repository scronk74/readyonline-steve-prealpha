Under 13, with a grown-up

Three steps for when something online feels wrong. Best read together. Reading on your own? That's okay. The three steps still work.

Option A: Read together

Read this together

For the grown-up: the reading takes about two minutes. The talk can take as long as you like. Read it aloud, or take turns. One thing matters most: your child knows they can tell you about anything online, and that you will stay calm.

Read aloud:

Games, videos, chatting with friends: being online is fun. Sometimes something feels wrong. You don't have to work it out by yourself. Here is what to do.

  1. Pause. Stop. Don't tap. Don't type back.

  2. Think. Is someone rushing me? Do they want a secret? Are they asking for something of mine, like my password, my address or a picture of me?

  3. Act. Tell a grown-up you trust.

Talk about it. Take turns to answer.

  • What is your favourite thing to do online right now?

  • Has anything online ever felt weird or wrong?

  • Which two grown-ups could you tell?

Make a deal. Say it out loud.

  • Child: "If something online feels wrong, I'll tell you."

  • Grown-up: "If you tell me, I'll stay calm and listen first. You won't be in trouble for telling."

Option B: Story

Maya and the free coins

A made-up story.

Maya was building a castle in her favourite game. A new player joined. "Cool castle!" the player said. Maya smiled.

Then the player wrote: "Want 2,000 free coins? Just tell me your password. Quick! Don't tell your mom."

Maya's tummy felt funny. So she did three things.

She paused. She took her hands off the tablet. She didn't type back.

She thought. They're rushing me. They want a secret. They want my password.

She acted. She took the tablet to Grandpa. "Something in my game feels wrong," she said. "Can you help me?"

Grandpa stayed calm. He listened first. Then they blocked the player together, and Maya went back to her castle.

Maya's three steps are your three steps:

  1. Pause. Stop. Don't tap. Don't type back.

  2. Think. Is someone rushing me? Do they want a secret? Are they asking for something of mine?

  3. Act. Tell a grown-up you trust.

Your turn. Who would you tell? Pick a grown-up you trust. It could be a parent, a grandparent, a teacher or a coach.

13 to 14

New accounts, new people. How to spot pressure, and what to do.

Option A: The basics

You know the apps. Most of what happens there is fine. This is about the moment a chat stops being fun and starts being a push.

Three signs it's pressure

  • Hurry. "Do it now or you'll miss out." A real offer gives you time to check.

  • Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone." Secrecy is a red flag, even when someone is kind.

  • Owing. "After everything I gave you." A gift never means you owe someone access. A streak is not a promise you owe someone.

One sign is enough to pause.

Pause · Think · Act

Pause. You don't have to answer right now. You do not need proof before you pause.

Think. Who is really asking? What do they want: a photo, a password, a code, money?

Act. Pick a move:

  • Stop replying. You can leave without explaining yourself.

  • Check another way. Message the real person, or go to the real site yourself.

  • Block and report the account.

  • Tell someone you trust. They can help you choose the next step.

Someone you trust

Pick someone who stays calm and listens before they react. A parent is a good pick. So is an aunt, an uncle, a teacher, a coach, or an older brother or sister.

Ways to start:

  • "Can I show you something on my phone? I'm not sure about it."

  • "Something happened online. Can you just listen first?"

If the first person doesn't help, try someone else.

Option B: Story

The follower

A made-up story, built from situations that are common online.

Zoe is 13. She has had her account for two months.

A new follower likes ten of her posts in a row. Then a message: "Your art is so good. You should have way more followers." It feels nice. Nothing is wrong yet.

They talk every day. A streak starts. Then: "Let's use my private story. Don't tell your friends, they'd be jealous."

Sign: secrecy.

A few days later: "I sent you that gift card. You could at least send me a pic. Just for me."

Sign: owing.

Zoe doesn't answer. An hour later: "Hello?? Answer now or I'm gone, and the streak dies."

Sign: hurry.

Zoe reads the chat again. It doesn't feel the way it did two weeks ago. So she pauses, and she sends nothing. She shows the chat to her aunt. Her aunt stays calm and listens. Then they block and report the account together.

The follower was kind first. That is how pressure usually starts. Being kind doesn't cancel the signs.

What Zoe did

  • Pause. She didn't answer straight away. You do not need proof before you pause.

  • Think. Who is really asking, and what do they want?

  • Act. She stopped replying and told someone she trusts.

If Zoe had already sent a photo, her next step would be the same: stop replying and tell someone she trusts. It would not be her fault.

See it happen: the free skin

A made-up chat from a game. Hints point out each sign as it shows up. Two to three minutes.

  1. A nice message.

    Kai_plays, someone you met in a match last week, writes: "gg! you're actually good. i've got a spare skin code. want it?"

    Hint: Nothing is wrong yet. Just notice who this is: someone you only know from the game.

    What this shows: Kind is fine. Kind doesn't tell you who someone is.

  2. A move, and a secret.

    Kai_plays: "dm me on the other app, it's easier. don't say anything in team chat, the others will want one too"

    Hint: Two things just changed. Where does Kai want to talk? Who isn't supposed to know?

    What this shows: Secrecy is a red flag, even when someone is kind. A private chat takes away the people who could help you notice pressure.

  3. A clock, and an ask.

    Kai_plays: "need your login for 2 min to add the skin. quick, the code runs out in 10"

    Hint: Who set the 10-minute clock? And why would a gift need your login?

    What this shows: Hurry is a sign. A real offer gives you time to check. A real gift doesn't need your password.

  4. Pause.

    You stop typing. You don't have to answer right now.

    Hint: Count the signs so far: secrecy and hurry. One is enough to pause, and you have two.

    What this shows: You do not need proof before you pause.

  5. Think.

    Who is really asking, and what do they want? Kai would get your account. You might get nothing.

    Hint: If the skin were real, Kai could send you the code and you could type it in yourself.

    What this shows: Thinking takes ten seconds. Ask who gains.

  6. Act.

    Your moves: stop replying, check another way, block and report, or tell someone you trust. You write "no thanks" and stay in team chat.

    Hint: Any of these moves works. Pick the one you can do right now.

    What this shows: You choose the move. You can leave without explaining yourself.

  7. The push back.

    Kai_plays: "wow. i was trying to be nice. thought we were friends"

    Hint: That's the third sign. Kai gave you something, and now says you owe something.

    What this shows: A gift never means you owe someone access. A push after you say no is one more reason to go.

  8. Out. And if you had already sent it?

    You block and report Kai_plays. Later you tell your cousin what happened.

    Hint: If you had sent your login, you would change your password and tell someone you trust. It would not be your fault.

    What this shows: Three signs: hurry, secrecy, owing. One habit: Pause · Think · Act.

15 to 18

Pressure, fake accounts and threats: how to spot them and get out.

Option A: Read the moment

You know the apps. This is about reading the moment: when a chat, a DM or a game stops being fun and starts being pressure. Trouble online rarely looks like danger. It looks like someone pushing you to act fast.

You are not imagining the pressure. In ReadyOnline's 2026 survey of 80 people aged 14 to 22, 62% had seen a fake or copycat account, 52% had unwanted contact from a stranger and 42% had been targeted by a money scam.

Three signs it's pressure

  • Hurry. "Reply now or you'll miss out." A real offer gives you time to check.

  • Secrecy. "Don't tell anyone." Secrecy is a red flag, even when someone is kind.

  • Owing. A streak, a gift or a rank gets used to make you feel you owe something. A streak is not a promise you owe someone. A gift never means you owe someone access.

The app can change. Pressure patterns stay the same.

Pause · Think · Act

Pause. You don't have to answer right now. You do not need proof before you pause.

Think. Who is really asking, and what do they want: a photo, a password, a code, money? A new account can look polished and still be false. Voices, photos and videos can be faked too. A real brand does not need your password, code, or private photos.

Act. Pick the move that keeps you in control:

  • Stop replying. You can leave without explaining yourself.

  • Check another way. Message the real person, or go to the official site yourself.

  • Block and report the account.

  • Talk to someone you trust.

Option B: The play

How a pressure play runs

Fake offers, account scams and people who want photos mostly run the same play. Here it is, step by step. You can walk away at any step.

  1. The opener. A compliment, a shared game, a "brand deal". It feels good, and nothing is wrong yet. A flattering comment can be a way to get access.

  2. The gift. A code, a skin, money, attention. Later it gets called a debt. A gift never means you owe someone access.

  3. The move. "Let's talk somewhere else." A private channel can remove the people who could help you notice pressure.

  4. The secret. "Don't tell anyone." Secrecy is a red flag, even when someone is kind.

  5. The ask. A photo, a password, a code, money.

  6. The clock. "Now, or else." A real offer gives you time to check.

Your move, at any step

Pause. You do not need proof before you pause.

Think. Which step is this? Who is really asking, and what do they want?

Act. Stop replying, check another way, block and report, or talk to someone you trust.

Getting out at step 2 is easier than at step 5. But it is never too late to get out, and it is never your fault that someone ran a play on you.

Option C: For a friend

When it's happening to a friend

A friend may tell you before they tell any adult. You don't have to fix it. You can help them pause, and help them find someone who can.

The signs are the same for them as for you: hurry, secrecy, owing.

Say:

  • "I'm with you. Let's find someone calm to help us think through this."

  • "This is not your fault."

Do:

  • Listen first. Skip "Why did you…?"

  • Help them pause: no more replies, no payments, nothing more sent.

  • Help them keep the messages and the account name.

  • Go with them to someone they trust, or to Get help.

Don't:

  • Don't message the other person or try to catch them out.

  • Don't pass screenshots or images around.

  • Don't promise to keep it secret. Keeping it secret can make pressure stronger. Try: "I won't tell everyone. But we need one person who can help."

In ReadyOnline's 2026 survey of 80 people aged 14 to 22, 76% said knowing they won't be judged would help them ask for help. You can be the person who doesn't judge.

And when it's you

The same habit works: Pause · Think · Act. Pause before you answer. Think about who is really asking and what they want. Act: stop replying, check another way, block and report, or talk to someone you trust.

Carrying a friend's problem is heavy, too. You can ask for help for yourself.

If it's already gone further

Maybe you replied, clicked, sent something or paid. You can still get out, and you still deserve help. Someone used pressure or a trick on you. That's on them.

  • Shared a password or a login code? Change that password now.

  • Sent money? Go to I think I've been scammed.

  • Someone is making threats? Go to If someone threatens to share a photo or video of you.

Bringing someone in

Pick someone who will stay calm and listen first. It doesn't have to be a parent. A coach, a teacher, a counselor, a relative, an older sibling or a friend you trust all count.

Ways to start:

  • "Something happened online. Can you just listen first?"

  • "I need help with something online, and I need you to stay calm."

If the first person doesn't help, try someone else, or go to Get help.

If someone threatens to share a photo or video of you

Someone has a nude or private photo or video of you, real or faked, or says they do. They say they'll share it unless you pay or send more. This is blackmail. It's often called sextortion. It can happen to anyone.

  1. Don't pay, and don't send more. Giving in rarely makes it stop.

  2. Stop replying.

  3. Keep the messages and the account name. Don't delete the chat. Don't save or forward the images.

  4. Block the account and report it in the app.

  5. Tell someone you trust, or go to Get help.

  6. Know this: it is not your fault. The person threatening you is the one doing wrong.

You don't need perfect proof to ask for help. You don't have to explain everything first.
